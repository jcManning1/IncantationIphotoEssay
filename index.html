Incantation I Photo Essay

Abstract

This photo essay explores the quiet and difficult realities of mental health. It focuses on isolation, habits, and contradictions that can exist beneath the surface of one's life. This work considers struggles that people carry that remain invisible to the world around them through intimate and unsettling moments. Everyone has a quiet struggle, you are not alone.

Project Description

This photo essay examines the experiences of living with mental health struggles within the ordinary spaces of life everyday. Rather than depicting mental health as something that is entirely separated from normal life, this project examines how it lives alongside our routines, relationships, work, and isolation. These photos move between quiet observations and direct representations of distress, creating a visual contrast between the visible struggle and the invisible struggle.

The project is site-specific in its relationship to NYC. The city provides the physical setting and social environments in which these photos exist. NYC's overwhelming density, constant movement, anonymity, and scale provide a backdrop for the consideration of isolation and disconnection. The contrast of public spaces and intimate environments allows the work to explore how someone can feel alone while being surrounded by millions of people. NYC is not just a prop, it's an active part of the projects meaning. The final work is intended to be presented through possibe projections in public space, and through a website that allows the photographs to exist as a sequence that the audience can move through at their own pace.

The path-marking is the first attempt to turn a lived passage into a reproducible route. The failure to document the route is the point. Some realities will survive the format that would make them public. This is why the piece talks artists who works with images, recordings, installations, archives, or platforms. Media is not only how we show a world, media is often how a world gets found, named, and then lost again, because naming attracts the wrong kind of attention.

The intended audience is very broad. In particular, it's meant for people who have experienced mental health difficulties themselves, people who have supported someone struggling with them, and those who may have little understanding of the subject. This project will not function as a clinical explanation of mental illness or as an attemp to speak for everyone who has experienced/experiences this. The goal is to create recognition, encourage reflection, and encouragement to seek help. The work will provoke the viewers to consider how much of another person's struggle can actually be seen.

Visual metaphors play an important role throught the project. Repetition, empty space, shadows, reflections, physical barriers, confined environments, and figures surrounded by crowds can suggest emotional distance, isolation, or the experience of being trapped in your own thoughts. Behaviors and objects associated with coping, addiction, avoidance, and routine also function as metaphors of struggle that are too abstract to communicate directly. Everyday environments appear often, but their context can change. This suggests how familiar places can be experienced differently, depending on a one's mental state.

Ultimately, the project is concerned with the gap between appearance and experience. Mental health struggles are often invisible, and the photos attempt to make the invisibility tangible without reducing the subject to a single narrative. By placing moments of discomfort alongside ordinary spaces, the project provokes viewers to look more closely at what might otherwise go unnoticed.
